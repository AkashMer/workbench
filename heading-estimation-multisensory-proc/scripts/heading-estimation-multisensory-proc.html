Undecided

Multisensory processing occurs via integration across sensory modalities. But like any other system, our sensory apparatus adds noise to the incoming information. How are we able to perceive things so smoothly despite the uncertainty in the information received? Bayesian inference has been pegged as one of the possible mechanisms our brain uses to solve this intractable problem [@Noppeney_2021; @Kording_2007]. In addition to the noise generated by the sensory apparatus, the brain’s computational resources are biologically constrained. Bayesian inference allows the internal processes in the brain to weigh the sensory information based on its reliability. Reliabilities provide a link between the internal model of the world and reality. Thus the weights are capturing the relationships which occur in the real world ie. the causal structure of the world around us. Integration of suboptimal information coming from different senses shows characteristics of normative (rule-based) bayesian framework [@Noppeney_2021]. One way to study this integration across modalities is to ask how does the brain differentiate if the information received is coming from common or independent sources ie. the binding problem. Spatial cognition is a prime example of the binding problem, especially in a naturalistic environment. My main goal is to apply and confirm the mechanisms described in a multisensory integration review paper [@Noppeney_2021] to heading estimation task performed in VR [@Harootonian_2022].

Experiment Design

33 participants wore VR headsets with controllers held in both hands in a rectangular room. Each participant performed ~400 trials for ~90 minutes. The sessions were divided into two blocks separated by a 2-10 minute break. The data from 3 participants is not present in the public dataset. These were excluded by the experimenters due to motion sickness.

Trial and Block Structure

Each trial followed the following structure:

  • Encoding period: Participant faces a door on the VR screen. They rotate around the vertical axis towards the controller which vibrates in the right or left hand. The stop of the vibrations signals the participant has arrived to a target angular rotation.
  • Retrieval period: The VR screen goes blank and the participant is asked to rotate back to re-face the door. The direction choice for turning back was left to the participants. There were two types of retrieval periods:
    • noFb trials: No visual feedback is provided
    • FB trials: ~ 300 msec flash of the virtual environment is provided as feedback while they are turning back to re-face the door
  • Response period: Participants press a button on the controller to record their response. No time limits were set.
  • Subjective rating period: Paticipants adjust a red triangle’s angle to indicate their confidence.

Each of the two blocks were divided into:

  • Practice period: 10 trials
  • No Feedback period: 25 trials
  • Feedback period: 150 trials
  • No Feedback period: 25 trials

Ethological significance

The trial structure reminded me of when I would enter a room, grab something and leave through the door all the while focusing on my phone. Humans are able to perform this simple task with great fidelity even in novel environments. The immersive nature of the VR, free-choice for direction during the retrieval period and no time limits add to the ethological relevance of the experiment. The subjective rating period provides a reset time for the participants and may reduce the effects due to learning. Can adding a response action of opening the door in the virtual environment increase the ethological relevance?

dataconf.csv contains the following columns:

  • subj: Participant identifier; 30 participants in total
  • trial: Trial identifier; some trials were excluded from the data when the participant rotated in the wrong direction during encoding period, pressed the wrong response button or responded before receiving the visual feedback
  • target (degrees): Angle value where the controller vibration stops ranging from 0 to 360; circularly uniform and anchored to the door
  • respond (degrees): Total angle the participant turns during the retrieval period ie. accumulates before the response
  • error (degrees): respond - target; objective measure of performance
  • condition: Marks FB vs. noFB trials
  • fb (degrees): Angular position of the feedback shown; anchored to the door
  • fb_true (degrees): True angular position of the feedback; anchored to the door
  • fb_offset (degrees): fb - fb_true; signifies the reliability of the visual feedback. These were generated in two ways:
    • 70% of the trials: \(\sim Gaussian(0, 30)\)
    • 30% of the trials: \(\sim Uniform(-180, 180)\)
  • fb_time (msec): Marks the time from trial start to feedback flash
  • trial_duration (msec): Total time taken by the participant to press the response button from trial start
  • conf (degrees): Response confidence level; subjective measure of performance
  • viewAmount (degrees): View angle traversed by the participant while the visual feedback was visible; signifies how much attention was payed to the feedback. For eg., a lower viewAmount would suggest that the participant slowed down to reorient themselves to the new context obtained from the feedback

# Is respond's variance coming directly from error rather than target?
# ie. check if the last term in Var(respond) = var(target) + var(error) + 2 cov(target, error) -> 0
# Repeated measures correlation controls for subject to avoid pooling across subjects
respond_error_rmcorr <- rmcorr(subj, target, error, dataset = train)
  • Respond angle increases monotonically with target, consistent with respond = target + error relationship (Figure 2 (a)). The width of the bootstrapped (1000) 95% confidence interval (CI) also increases with target angle suggesting an increase in uncertainity with increasing difficulty of the task.
  • Median error is consistently positive (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001; bootstrapped median 95% CI [10.47, 23.42]) but does not increase or decrease as a function of target angle (bootstrapped median regression slope 95% CI [-0.027, 0.058]). However, the width of the bootstrapped (1000) 95% CI for mean error increases with target angle just like respond (Figure 2 (b)).
  • The growing uncertainty in respond tracks error directly, since:

\[ \begin{aligned} &respond = target + error \\ \implies &Var(respond) = Var(target) + Var(error) + 2\;Cov(target, error) \end{aligned} \]

The repeated measures correlation between target and error is negligible (\(r\) = 0.032, 95% CI [0.009, 0.054]). Thus, error can be used as the predicting variable instead of respond to model participant’s response.

  • Median error was higher in trials where visual feedback was provided (bootstrapped 95% CI of difference between two conditions = [2.18, 6.84]\(^\circ\)), consistent with varying reliability of the visual feedback (Figure 3 (a)). The above deviation from the forced fusion model based on a normative Bayesian observer has been shown before where the observers have a tendency to overweight signals from a sensory modality (vision) which can provide better information than their own proprioceptive sense [@Noppeney_2021].
  • Median error is low for reliable visual feedback (low offsets), increases rapidly for positive offsets till around 60\(^\circ\) and decreases thereafter (Figure 3 (b)). On the other hand, error increases gradually for negative offsets, plateaus around 60\(^\circ\), then increases again (Figure 3 (b)). Thus, participants’ responses may be affected by the direction of the feedback offset (mirrored positive vs. negative bins, bootstrapped 95% CI of difference = [3.53, 14.15]\(^\circ\)) along with it’s reliability. The extent of integration between proprioception and vision depends on the size of the conflict between the information from both sources [@Noppeney_2021].

Main goal was to create a generalizable linear model which can track it’s own uncertainity, offering an alternative to the nonlinear path integration and cue combination models defined in the source paper [@Harootonian_2022]. GAMLSS [@R.A.Rigby_2005] was chosen since the scale (\(\sigma\)) and shape (\(\nu\)) can be modelled against task variables. This was necessary here to capture how uncertainity scales with task difficulty, presence or absence of feedback, reliability of the feedback and other time-related variables. The skew-normal (SN2) distribution family is used, since error’s median is less than it’s mean even across subjects (bootstrapped 95% CI for median(error) - mean(error) [-4.847, -0.662]\(^\circ\)). Each model’s predictors were tuned based on 5-fold cross validation across the training set containing data from 24 participants.

Unisensory Model

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{error} &\sim SN2(\mu, \sigma, \nu) \\ \mu &= \beta_0 + b_{subj} \\ \sigma &= \gamma_0 + \gamma_1 \; \text{target} \\ \nu &= \delta_0 + \delta_1 \; \text{target} + \delta_2 \; \text{trial\_duration} \end{aligned} \]

# Define the number of cycles to run for modelling and non-verbose output
gamlss_control <- gamlss.control(n.cyc = 300, trace = FALSE)

# Define the model
# Internal representation of the rotation
unisensory_formula <- error ~ 1 + re(random = ~1|subj, level = 0) # subj as a random intercept
# Skew-normal distribution for error
unisensory_family <- SN2()

# Winning formulas from cross-validated comparison (cv-unisensory.rds)
# SD ie. noise: target only

# Skewness: target + trial_duration
unisensory_nu_formula <- ~ target + trial_duration + re(random = ~1|subj, level = 0)

# Fit the model

  formula = unisensory_formula,
  sigma.formula = unisensory_sigma_formula,
  nu.formula = unisensory_nu_formula,

  data = train,

This model assumes that all trials were performed using proprioceptive information without discounting the reliability of the visual feedback. The residuals are even closer to a normal distribution (Kolmogorov-Smirnov test D = 0.018, p = 0.008).

Hybrid Model

This model combines the unisensory and multisensory models’ predictions by weighting them according to their precisions [@Noppeney_2021]. The combined predicted SD of error is lower than either individual model’s prediction against target (?@fig-model-hybrid-1) and against offset magnitude (?@fig-model-hybrid-2). The hybrid model has reduced uncertainity as well.
