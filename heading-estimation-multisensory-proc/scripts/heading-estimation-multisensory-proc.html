Undecided

Multisensory processing occurs via integration across sensory modalities. But like any other system, our sensory apparatus adds noise to the incoming information. How are we able to perceive things so smoothly despite the uncertainty in the information received? Bayesian inference has been pegged as one of the possible mechanisms our brain uses to solve this intractable problem [@Noppeney_2021; @Kording_2007]. In addition to the noise generated by the sensory apparatus, the brain’s computational resources are biologically constrained. Bayesian inference allows the internal processes in the brain to weigh the sensory information based on its reliability. Reliabilities provide a link between the internal model of the world and reality. The weights capture the relationships which occur in the real world ie. the causal structure of the world around us. Integration of suboptimal information coming from different senses shows characteristics of normative (rule-based) Bayesian framework [@Noppeney_2021]. One way to study this integration across modalities is to ask how does the brain differentiate if the information received is coming from common or independent sources ie. the binding problem. Spatial cognition is a prime example of the binding problem, especially in a naturalistic environment. My main goal is to apply and confirm the mechanisms described in a multisensory integration review paper [@Noppeney_2021] to heading estimation task performed in VR [@Harootonian_2022].

Experiment Design

33 participants wore VR headsets with controllers held in both hands in a rectangular room. Each participant performed ~400 trials for ~90 minutes. The sessions were divided into two blocks separated by a 2-10 minute break. The data from 3 participants were excluded by the experimenters due to motion sickness.

Trial and Block Structure

Each trial followed the following structure:

  • Encoding period: Participant faces a door on the VR screen. They rotate around the vertical axis towards the controller which vibrates in the right or left hand. Participants are instructed to stop rotating when the vivrations stop indicating target angular rotation.
  • Retrieval period: The VR screen goes dark and the participant is asked to rotate back to re-face the door. The direction choice for turning back was left to the participants. There were two types of retrieval periods:
    • noFb trials: No visual feedback is provided
    • FB trials: ~ 300 msec flash of the virtual environment is provided as feedback while they are turning back to re-face the door
  • Response period: Participants press a button on the controller to record their response once they think they are facing the virtual door. No time limits were set.
  • Subjective rating period: Paticipants adjust a red triangle’s angle to indicate their confidence.

Each of the two blocks were divided into:

  • Practice period: 10 trials
  • No Feedback period: 25 trials
  • Feedback period: 150 trials
  • No Feedback period: 25 trials

Ethological significance

The trial structure reminded me of when I would enter a room, grab something and leave through the door all the while focusing on my phone. Humans are able to perform this simple task with great fidelity even in novel environments. The immersive nature of the VR, free-choice for direction during the retrieval period and no time limits add to the ethological relevance of the experiment. The subjective rating period provides a reset time for the participants and may reduce the effects due to learning. Can adding a response action of opening the door in the virtual environment increase the ethological relevance?

dataconf.csv contains the following columns:

  • subj: Participant identifier; 30 participants in total
  • trial: Trial identifier; some trials when the participant rotated in the wrong direction during encoding period, pressed the wrong response button or responded before receiving the visual feedback were excluded by the experimentors
  • target (degrees): Angle value at which the controller vibration stops ranging from 0 to 360; circularly uniform and anchored to the door
  • respond (degrees): Total angle the participant turns during the retrieval period ie. accumulates before the response
  • error (degrees): respond - target; objective measure of performance
  • condition: Marks FB vs. noFB trials
  • fb (degrees): Angular position of the feedback shown; anchored to the door
  • fb_true (degrees): True angular position of the feedback; anchored to the door
  • fb_offset (degrees): fb - fb_true; signifies the reliability of the visual feedback. These were generated in two ways:
    • 70% of the trials: \(\sim Gaussian(0, 30)\)
    • 30% of the trials: \(\sim Uniform(-180, 180)\)
  • fb_time (msec): Marks the time from trial start to feedback flash
  • trial_duration (msec): Total time taken by the participant to press the response button from trial start
  • conf (degrees): Response confidence level; subjective measure of performance
  • viewAmount (degrees): View angle traversed by the participant while the visual feedback was visible; signifies how much attention was payed to the feedback. For eg., a lower viewAmount would suggest that the participant slowed down to reorient themselves to the new context obtained from the feedback

# Is respond's variance coming directly from error rather than target?
# ie. check if the last term in Var(respond) = var(target) + var(error) + 2 cov(target, error) -> 0
# Repeated measures correlation controls for subject to avoid pooling across subjects
respond_error_rmcorr <- rmcorr(subj, target, error, dataset = data)
  • Respond angle increases monotonically with target, consistent with respond = target + error relationship (Figure 2 (a)). The width of the bootstrapped 95% confidence interval (CI) also increases with target angle suggesting an increase in uncertainity with increasing difficulty of the task.
  • Median error is consistently positive (Wilcoxon signed-rank test, p < 0.001; bootstrapped median 95% CI [10.89, 20.43]) but does not increase or decrease as a function of target angle (bootstrapped median regression slope 95% CI [-0.019, 0.052]). However, the width of the bootstrapped 95% CI for mean error increases with target angle just like respond (Figure 2 (b)).
  • The growing uncertainty in respond tracks error directly, since:

\[ \begin{aligned} &respond = target + error \\ \implies &Var(respond) = Var(target) + Var(error) + 2\;Cov(target, error) \end{aligned} \]

The repeated measures correlation between target and error is negligible (\(r\) = 0.028, 95% CI [0.009, 0.047]). Thus, error can be used as the predicting variable instead of respond to model participant’s response.

  • Median error was higher in trials where visual feedback was provided (bootstrapped 95% CI of difference between two conditions = [1.92, 6.28]\(^\circ\)), consistent with varying reliability of the visual feedback (Figure 3 (a)). The above deviation from the forced fusion model based on a normative Bayesian observer has been shown before where the observers have a tendency to overweight signals from a sensory modality (vision) which can provide better information than their own proprioceptive sense [@Noppeney_2021].
  • Median error is low for reliable visual feedback (low offsets), increases rapidly for positive offsets till around 60\(^\circ\) and decreases thereafter (Figure 3 (b)). On the other hand, median error increases gradually for negative offsets, plateaus around 60\(^\circ\), then increases again (Figure 3 (b)). Thus, participants’ responses may be affected by the direction of the feedback offset (mirrored positive vs. negative bins, bootstrapped 95% CI of difference = [5.27, 16.71]\(^\circ\)) along with it’s reliability. The extent of integration between proprioception and vision depends on the size of the conflict between the information from both sources [@Noppeney_2021].

Models

Generalized Additive Models for Location Scale and Shape (gamlss library in R) [@R.A.Rigby_2005] were chosen for modelling error since it allows modelling not only of the central tendency, but also of the other parameters of the distribution like the standard deviation. GAMLSS uses penalized maximum likelihood estimate (MLE) to fit models. A distribution family for the predicted variable can be selected from a very general family of distributions. A skew-t (SST) family fit the error distribution far better than a normal distribution (see @appendix). The same family was used for the following models. The shape parameters (skewness, \(\nu\) and kurtosis, \(\tau\)) were not modelled since the overall shape of the uncertainity is usually unique to each individual and may not change in the time period of the experiment. Every model controls for inter-participant variability by including the participants as random effects predictor. Penalized smooth functions were used for the predictors which require no assumption of the order of relationship and penalize overfitting due to noise.

Naive Unisensory Model

This model naively assumes that error depends only on the proprioceptive signal available from target and amount of time it takes to respond.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{error} &\sim SST(\mu, \sigma, \nu, \tau) \\ \mu &= f_1(\text{target}) + f_2(\text{trial\_duration}) + b_{subj} \\ \log(\sigma) &= f_3(\text{target}) + f_4(\text{trial\_duration}) + b_{subj} \end{aligned} \]

where each \(f_i\) is a penalized smooth function and \(b_{subj}\) is a random intercept for per-subject fit. No collinearity existed between the chosen predictors (Figure 8 (b)).

Cue Combination Model

A visual only model which assumes that error depends only on the visual signal available from target and the visual feedback’s reliability was built first.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{error} &\sim SST(\mu, \sigma, \nu, \tau) \\ \mu &= f_1(\text{target}) + f_2(\text{fb\_offset}) + b_{subj} \\ \log(\sigma) &= f_3(\text{target}) + f_4(\text{fb\_offset}) + b_{subj} \end{aligned} \]

The cue combination model combines the unisensory and visual only models’ predictions by weighting them according to their precisions [@Noppeney_2021] using the formula (assumes predicted error is Gaussian-like)[@Kording_2007]:

\[ \hat{\mu}_{combined} = \frac{\mu_{uni}/\sigma_{uni}^2 + \mu_{vis}/\sigma_{vis}^2}{1/\sigma_{uni}^2 + 1/\sigma_{vis}^2} \]

Flexible Multisensory Model

This model makes no assumptions about the distribution of the predict error or how the proprioceptive and visual signals are combined. A high-dimensional feature space is constructed from target, feedback reliability, amount of time spent to respond, the amount of visual information received (viewAmount). Interaction terms are included where coefficients for one variable (for example target representing proprioception) are allowed to change smoothly with the value of other variable (for example feedback offset representing vision) [@Hastie_1993]. Thus, the direction and magnitude of feedback offset acts as an effect modifier for target. This may resemble how integration of information takes place at the neuronal level via dendritic modulation of activity. The reparameterised skew-t family (SST()) selected was especially useful for defining these formulas since the SD of the prediction is represented by \(\sigma\) alone, unlike other skewed distrbutions (for example skew-normal) which compute SD of predictions using \(\sigma\) and \(\nu\) parameters.

\[ \begin{aligned} \text{error} &\sim SST(\mu, \sigma, \nu, \tau) \\ \mu &= f_1(\text{target}) + f_2(\text{trial\_duration}) + f_3(\text{fb\_offset}) + f_4(\text{viewAmount}) \\ &\quad + g_1(\text{target}, \text{fb\_offset}) + g_2(\text{trial\_duration}, \text{fb\_offset}) + g_3(\text{viewAmount}, \text{fb\_offset}) + b_{subj} \\ \log(\sigma) &= \text{(same structure as } \mu\text{)} \end{aligned} \]

where each \(g_i\) is a varying-coefficient smooth function for interactions between variables and their effect modifiers.

  dplyr::select(model, rmse) %>%

The Bayesian Information Criterion (BIC) score (penalizes complexity) was much lower for the flexible multisensory model than the naive unisensory model (Figure 11 (a)). A 5-fold cross validation of all 3 models showed the same ordering by average RMSE: naive unisensory, then cue combination, then flexible multisensory (Figure 11 (b)).

# Bootstrapped median regression slope of sd_col against a single predictor, controlling for subj
validation_slope <- function(sd_col, predictor, data) {
  b <- boot(subjs, function(d, i) {
    samp <- map_dfr(d[i], ~ data %>% filter(subj == .x))
    coef(rq(reformulate(predictor, response = sd_col), tau = 0.5, data = samp))[[2]]
  }, R = R_boot)
  boot.ci(b, type = "perc")$percent[4:5]
}

}

Predicted uncertainity for all three models tracks performance accuracy (Figure 12 (b), top), in the same manner as the subjective confidence measure. But naive unisensory and cue combination models’ predicted |error| decreases as attention increases (viewAmount) (Figure 12 (b), bottom). The flexible multisensory model instead shows predicted |error| increasing with viewAmount across all six feedback offset levels (Figure 12 (a)), consistent with the behavioral relationship of these variables.

In conclusion, the flexible multisensory model is behaviorally consistent and performs similarly to the cue combination model for integration between proprioception and vision. The cue combination model has been suggested as one of the mechanisms for the binding problem, but several suboptimalities have been indicated before [@Noppeney_2021], such as:

  • Observers arbitrarily switch between segregation and integration at a fixed conflict size, without accounting for their sensory uncertainities.
  • Integration deviated from normative fusion for small conflict sizes when the participants were asked to selectively attend and report their perception under one sensory modality. A complex interaction between multisensory processing and attention was attributed as one of the mechanisms for the deviation in this specific case.
  • The experimentors reported that a hybrid model which combined path integration from proprioception signal and cue combination performed better than individual models [@Harootonian_2022].

The flexible multisensory model presented above uses a different approach grounded in effect modulation between features which could mirror the dendritic modulation seen at neuronal level. The high dimensional feature space limits the interpretability of the model. But the internal representational structure of the brain may be similar to this high dimensional feature space. The compact size and modular nature of electroencephalogram (EEG) could be useful to design a similar experiment to elucidate this internal representational structure.

One thing is clear to me: the concepts and mechanisms of multisensory processing are applicable to spatial cognition questions and should be considered when investigating how the sense of space is built.
